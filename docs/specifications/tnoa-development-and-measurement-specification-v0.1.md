**Status:** Active / Provisional / Expansion Scaffold

---

## Overwrite and reinterpretation notice

This specification introduces **Normative Orientation Architecture** as the descriptive umbrella for values, principles, norms, ideology, morality, ethics, factual premises, motives, intentions, behavior, and developmental updating.

It does **not** delete the existing Karma model or personality taxonomy. It repositions them within a clearer architecture:

- Personality describes dispositional tendencies and characteristic forms of expression.
- TNOA describes how an individual constructs and applies judgments of requirement, permission, prohibition, justification, blame, entitlement, sacrifice, and responsibility.
- Karma remains a provisional ethical-review and evaluative overlay rather than the primary descriptive representation of normative identity.
- Reconciliation remains a mechanism for examining mismatches, ambiguity, competing explanations, and context without assuming error or moral failure.

Affected repository areas include the project overview, model manifest, Karma boundary language, specification index, and documentation workflows. Existing source material is preserved and remains reviewable.

# 1. Executive summary

TNOA is intended to answer:

> How does a person construct, prioritize, justify, and enact judgments about what is required, permitted, prohibited, excusable, praiseworthy, blameworthy, or desirable across contexts and over time?

The architecture must not begin by deciding whether a person is good or bad. Its first task is to reconstruct how conduct becomes justifiable to the individual. Ethical evaluation may be applied later through an explicitly declared benchmark.

The model therefore separates three layers:

1. **Descriptive layer:** What the person believes, values, permits, prohibits, intends, and excludes.
2. **Explanatory layer:** How personality, cognition, affect, development, relationships, learning, ideology, and ecology contribute to those orientations.
3. **Evaluative layer:** How the orientation compares with a stated ethical, legal, professional, cultural, or human-rights standard.

The core unit of analysis is the **Normative Decision Episode**, not a global morality score. A complete profile is assembled from repeated episodes across roles, relationships, audiences, ecological systems, levels of constraint, and time.

TNOA should be regarded as an integrative developmental identity architecture and hypothesis-generating framework until its constructs, measures, processes, and interpretations are empirically validated.

## 2.1 Evidence levels

TNOA adopts the Tessera evidence-classification approach.

| Level | Classification | Meaning for TNOA |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Empirical foundation | Established research domains may support individual variables or processes, such as values, norms, attitudes, moral judgment, intention, social influence, learning, memory, stress, and developmental context. |
| 2 | Integrative framework | TNOA combines supported constructs into an organized decision architecture. The integration may be theoretically justified without yet being validated as one unified model. |
| 3 | Tessera hypothesis | New graph structures, composite variables, automated inference, profile synthesis, contextual coefficients, and developmental update rules require direct testing. |

## 2.2 Evidence rules

- Empirical findings must not be overwritten merely to simplify the model.
- A construct supported in one research context must not be assumed valid in every culture, age group, or application.
- Source-informed does not mean psychometrically validated as a Tessera measure.
- Machine-readable does not mean inferentially reliable.
- The model must permit `unknown`, `not assessed`, `not applicable`, and `insufficient evidence` states.
- Every inference should preserve its source, date, context, confidence, and alternative explanations.
- No single observation should be converted into a stable identity claim without sufficient repeated evidence.

# 3. Governing construct

## 3.1 Formal definition

**Normative orientation** is the patterned way an individual assigns authority, priority, permission, obligation, prohibition, entitlement, praise, blame, excuse, and responsibility to possible actions, relationships, outcomes, institutions, identities, and social arrangements.

**Normative Orientation Architecture** is the organized system of values, principles, norms, ideologies, factual premises, moralized concerns, ethical reasoning modes, motives, intentions, contextual conditions, and developmental processes through which those assignments are produced and revised.

## 3.2 Foundational semantic distinction

The architecture distinguishes:

- **Values:** Desired ends, conditions, relationships, identities, or states of affairs.
- **Principles:** Generalized rules that translate values into judgments.
- **Norms:** Perceived standards of expected, approved, or role-appropriate conduct.
- **Ideology:** A larger worldview connecting social categories, history, authority, causality, identity, threat, legitimacy, and prescriptions for social order.
- **Morality:** Judgments experienced as possessing special force involving obligation, prohibition, entitlement, praise, blame, or deserved sanction.
- **Ethics:** Reflective or systematized organization of normative judgments and reasons.
- **Factual premises:** Beliefs about what is true, likely, causal, threatening, beneficial, or historically relevant.
- **Motives:** Desired outcomes or states energizing action.
- **Intentions:** Selected commitments to attempt particular actions.
- **Behavior:** Enacted or omitted conduct under actual opportunities and constraints.
- **Consequences:** Intended, foreseen, unforeseen, perceived, and actual outcomes.

## 3.3 What morality means in this architecture

Morality is not equated with compassion, harmlessness, conformity, productivity, legality, or social approval. These may become moral contents, but none defines morality by itself.

At its most foundational, morality is a system through which an agent gives certain reasons special normative force over conduct and treatment. An issue becomes moralized when it is experienced not merely as liked, useful, customary, or legal, but as required, forbidden, deserved, blameworthy, praiseworthy, or universally binding.

A coherent, internalized moral system may support humane conduct or harmful ideology. Internalization, conviction, complexity, consistency, and cost-bearing commitment are descriptive properties, not proof of ethical soundness.

# 4. Scope and non-goals

## 4.1 In scope

TNOA will represent:

- normative content;
- judgment structure;
- context-sensitive activation;
- developmental provenance;
- public-private divergence;
- intention-behavior divergence;
- factual-premise dependence;
- responsibility and excuse models;
- harm and beneficiary structure;
- revisability and identity protection;
- ecological and historical change;
- evidence confidence and uncertainty.

## 4.2 Out of scope for initial development

TNOA will not initially provide:

- a global good-versus-evil score;
- diagnosis of moral character;
- proof of hidden intent;
- automatic conversion from personality traits to morality;
- automatic criminal, clinical, employment, or legal conclusions;
- universal rankings of cultures or ideologies;
- deterministic prediction of behavior;
- claims that articulate reasoning necessarily produces ethical action;
- claims that social conformity demonstrates internal moral endorsement.

# 5. Layered architecture

## 5.1 Level A: Normative source systems

These provide the content from which judgments are constructed:

- values;
- principles;
- norms;
- ideologies and worldviews;
- moralized concerns;
- ethical frameworks;
- identity commitments;
- factual and causal beliefs;
- role expectations;
- narratives of history, threat, legitimacy, and belonging.

## 5.2 Level B: Decision-forming processes

These determine what becomes active in an episode:

- perception and situation construction;
- schema and association activation;
- affective appraisal;
- value activation;
- principle selection;
- norm recognition;
- authority recognition;
- factual-premise retrieval;
- outcome forecasting;
- beneficiary and harm representation;
- responsibility attribution;
- justification;
- deontic classification;
- motive competition.

## 5.3 Level C: Action formation

These determine whether orientation becomes conduct:

- intention strength;
- implementation planning;
- perceived behavioral control;
- capability;
- opportunity;
- role power;
- constraint load;
- habit and behavioral momentum;
- audience and social pressure;
- risk and sanction expectations;
- actual execution or omission.

## 5.4 Level D: Developmental updating

These explain how an episode changes the system:

- consequence evaluation;
- reinforcement or punishment;
- guilt, shame, pride, resentment, relief, fear, or moral elevation;
- social approval, rejection, or exclusion;
- narrative revision;
- identity consolidation or threat;
- value reprioritization;
- ideological reinforcement or disconfirmation;
- principle modification;
- accessibility shift;
- associative reweighting;
- constraint formation or relaxation.

## 5.5 Core processing sequence

```text
Person-in-context
-> Perception and situation construction
-> Factual and causal interpretation
-> Value, norm, ideology, and identity activation
-> Principle or justificatory mode selection
-> Deontic judgment
-> Motive competition
-> Intention and plan
-> Behavior under capability, opportunity, and constraint
-> Consequence
-> Appraisal, narrative integration, and developmental update
```

The architecture treats the person and context as a dynamic unit. Context is not a decorative variable attached after a judgment has already been scored.

# 6. Bioecological organization

TNOA uses the Process-Person-Context-Time model as a developmental organizing frame.

## 6.1 Process

Proximal processes are recurring interactions through which normative structures become practiced and internalized. Examples include:

- caregiver discipline and explanation;
- conflict and repair;
- peer inclusion and exclusion;
- role modeling;
- religious ritual;
- school discipline;
- workplace incentives;
- political discussion;
- exposure to media narratives;
- participation in institutions;
- repeated encounters with authority, scarcity, privilege, or threat.

## 6.2 Person

Person characteristics include:

- temperament and personality;
- cognitive and linguistic capacity;
- attachment expectations;
- affective responsiveness;
- executive control;
- social identity;
- prior learning;
- health and stress state;
- perceived agency;
- capabilities and vulnerabilities.

These characteristics affect exposure, interpretation, elicited responses from others, and the resources available for reflection. They do not determine normative content by themselves.

## 6.3 Context

### Microsystem

Immediate face-to-face settings such as family, peers, school, religious community, workplace, intimate relationships, and local groups.

### Mesosystem

Relations among microsystems. Contradictory signals between family, school, workplace, peer group, and community may produce compartmentalization, conflict, or selective rule application.

### Exosystem

Settings that affect the individual indirectly, including employment conditions, media systems, institutional policy, neighborhood resources, legal structures, and the decisions of powerful organizations.

### Macrosystem

Cultural narratives, ideologies, economic arrangements, social hierarchies, religious cosmologies, law, historical memory, and accepted models of personhood and authority.

### Chronosystem

Developmental timing, cohort, historical events, transitions, political change, technological change, migration, trauma, conversion, and cumulative exposure.

## 6.4 Time

The model distinguishes:

- microtime: what happens within an episode;
- mesotime: how patterns repeat across days, months, or recurring roles;
- macrotime: developmental, cohort, and historical change.

A complete profile must therefore include both stable tendencies and within-person variation.

# 7. Construct development program

Every umbrella element must be developed as an independent workstream before holistic scoring or automated synthesis is attempted.

## 7.1 Workstream 1: Values

### Core question

What ends, states, relationships, identities, or conditions does the person regard as important?

### Required distinctions

- abstract values versus concrete goals;
- desired values versus enacted values;
- personal versus collective values;
- intrinsic versus instrumental values;
- stable importance versus momentary salience;
- declared importance versus trade-off priority;
- identity-central versus peripheral values;
- protected versus negotiable values.

### Measurement candidates

- open value elicitation;
- ranking and best-worst scaling;
- forced trade-offs;
- resource-allocation tasks;
- narrative examples;
- ecological momentary sampling;
- value-conflict scenarios;
- beneficiary substitution.

### Output

A **value hierarchy network** containing centrality, contextual activation, conflicts, compatibility, beneficiaries, sacrifice thresholds, and developmental change.

## 7.2 Workstream 2: Principles

### Core question

What generalized rules does the person use to translate values into judgments?

### Required distinctions

- absolute versus defeasible principles;
- universal versus role-specific principles;
- positive duties versus prohibitions;
- procedural versus substantive principles;
- publicly stated versus privately applied principles;
- genuine rules versus post hoc rationalization;
- self-directed versus other-directed standards.

### Measurement candidates

- rule elicitation;
- matched scenarios;
- exception probing;
- counterexample testing;
- reversal tests;
- self-other symmetry tests;
- in-group/out-group substitution;
- consistency across domains.

### Output

A **principle application map** with trigger conditions, exceptions, priority, intended scope, actual scope, and conflict-resolution rules.

## 7.3 Workstream 3: Norms

### Core question

What standards does the person perceive as socially expected, approved, or role appropriate?

### Required distinctions

- descriptive norms: what people usually do;
- injunctive norms: what people approve or demand;
- personal norms: what the individual believes they ought to do;
- role norms: what is expected from a parent, citizen, leader, employee, believer, or group member;
- institutional norms: what organizations reward or punish;
- compliance versus endorsement;
- resistance versus concealment.

### Measurement candidates

- perceived norm estimates;
- approval estimates;
- sanction expectation;
- anonymous versus identified response;
- audience manipulation;
- authority observation;
- dissent availability;
- private agreement and desire-for-change probes.

### Output

A **normative pressure profile** showing internal endorsement, strategic compliance, fear-based compliance, role conformity, resistance, and public-private divergence.

## 7.4 Workstream 4: Ideology and worldview

### Core question

What larger system connects beliefs about reality, identity, history, authority, groups, causes, and preferred social order?

### Required components

- social categories;
- group identities;
- historical narratives;
- causal explanations;
- threat models;
- legitimacy claims;
- authority structures;
- value hierarchies;
- sacred commitments;
- prescriptions for social organization;
- narratives of enemies, victims, heroes, and decline;
- models of human nature.

### Required distinctions

- content versus strength;
- coherence versus factual accuracy;
- identity commitment versus reflective endorsement;
- institutional ideology versus personal adaptation;
- symbolic affiliation versus operational belief;
- ideology versus isolated prejudice;
- ideology versus religion, while allowing overlap.

### Output

An **ideological meaning network** connecting beliefs, values, identities, authorities, threats, prescriptions, and exception rules.

## 7.5 Workstream 5: Morality and moralization

### Core question

Which issues does the person experience as possessing special normative force?

### Required distinctions

- preference versus moral conviction;
- convention versus morality;
- prudential concern versus moral prohibition;
- sacred concern versus interpersonal concern;
- legal prohibition versus moral condemnation;
- disgust versus wrongdoing;
- identity threat versus moral violation;
- strong attitude versus perceived universal obligation.

### Measurement candidates

- wrong-versus-undesirable probes;
- authority-removal tests;
- consensus-removal tests;
- legality-change tests;
- harm-removal tests;
- punishment and tolerance judgments;
- perceived universality;
- willingness to impose the rule on others;
- willingness to bear cost.

### Output

A **moralization map** showing domains, strength, perceived objectivity, sanction support, universality, and reversibility.

## 7.6 Workstream 6: Ethics and justificatory repertoire

### Core question

What consciously articulated modes does the person use to organize or defend normative judgments?

### Candidate modes

- consequence-oriented;
- rule-oriented;
- rights-oriented;
- virtue and character-oriented;
- care and relationship-oriented;
- contractual and reciprocity-oriented;
- sacred or divine-command-oriented;
- role and professional ethics;
- precedent and tradition;
- necessity or emergency reasoning;
- particularistic case reasoning.

### Measurement candidates

- dilemma interviews;
- justification coding;
- rule-consequence conflicts;
- relationship-universality conflicts;
- role comparisons;
- think-aloud protocols;
- confidence and revisability ratings;
- alternative-justification generation.

### Output

A **justificatory repertoire profile**, not a single stage or philosophical type.

## 7.7 Workstream 7: Factual and epistemic premises

### Core question

What does the person believe is true about the situation, actors, risks, groups, causes, and likely outcomes?

### Required distinctions

- observed versus inferred belief;
- testimonial versus directly experienced belief;
- stereotype versus individuated evidence;
- probability estimate versus categorical claim;
- causal assumption versus association;
- historical claim;
- metaphysical or religious claim;
- confidence versus accuracy;
- evidence standard;
- source trust;
- willingness to update.

### Measurement candidates

- premise elicitation;
- source tracing;
- confidence judgments;
- calibration tasks;
- evidence-update tasks;
- counterevidence response;
- causal diagramming;
- ambiguity manipulation.

### Output

An **epistemic premise graph** attached to each judgment. The model may describe coherence relative to premises without endorsing those premises as accurate.

## 7.8 Workstream 8: Motives

### Core question

What immediate or intermediate outcome does the person want?

### Candidate motives

- protection;
- affiliation;
- status;
- control;
- revenge;
- care;
- justice;
- order;
- purity;
- security;
- approval;
- shame avoidance;
- material gain;
- group advancement;
- identity confirmation;
- meaning preservation;
- autonomy;
- relief from distress.

### Required distinctions

- conscious versus nonconscious;
- proximal versus distal;
- self-reported versus inferred;
- primary versus secondary gain;
- mixed motives;
- stable motive tendency versus episode-specific motive;
- moral motive versus strategic motive.

### Output

A **motive competition profile** showing activation, priority, inhibition, conflict, and retrospective reconstruction.

## 7.9 Workstream 9: Intentions

### Core question

What action has the person committed to attempting?

### Required distinctions

- desire versus intention;
- inclination versus commitment;
- abstract intention versus implementation plan;
- immediate versus delayed intention;
- conditional versus unconditional intention;
- private versus declared intention;
- prospective report versus retrospective claim;
- intention strength versus perceived control.

### Measurement candidates

- action commitment ratings;
- implementation-intention prompts;
- time horizon;
- means and contingency planning;
- perceived control;
- opportunity manipulation;
- follow-up behavior tracking.

### Output

An **intention specification** containing intended act, target, beneficiary, outcome, foreseen side effects, accepted costs, timing, perceived control, and commitment strength.

## 7.10 Workstream 10: Behavior and consequences

### Core question

What was enacted or omitted, under which opportunities and constraints, and what followed?

### Required distinctions

- attempted versus completed behavior;
- voluntary versus coerced behavior;
- habitual versus deliberative behavior;
- public performance versus private enactment;
- commission versus omission;
- capability failure versus intention failure;
- immediate versus delayed consequence;
- intended versus unintended outcome;
- perceived versus actual outcome.

### Output

An **enactment profile** linking orientation, intention, control, opportunity, behavior, consequence, and later revision.

# 8. Cross-cutting descriptive axes

The following dimensions are coding axes applied to episodes. They are not assumed to be fixed personality traits.

| Axis | Central question |
|---|---|
| Normative scope | Who or what receives consideration or standing? |
| Concern priority | Which values, interests, identities, or institutions dominate? |
| Deontic assignment | Is the act required, permitted, optional, discouraged, or forbidden? |
| Authority locus | What grants the judgment legitimacy? |
| Justificatory form | What type of reason supports the judgment? |
| Epistemic premise structure | What must be believed for the judgment to make sense? |
| Beneficiary orientation | Who is intended to gain or be protected? |
| Harm and means tolerance | What costs, coercion, or harms are accepted, and toward whom? |
| Responsibility model | How are control, intention, foreseeability, excuse, blame, praise, and repair assigned? |
| Internalization and conviction | Is the judgment externally imposed, personally endorsed, or identity-central? |
| Contextual modulation | Which changes in role, audience, target, power, threat, scarcity, or institution alter it? |
| Revisability | What evidence, experience, or reflection could change it? |

These axes should support continuous, categorical, network, and qualitative representations where appropriate. They should not be summed into a global morality score by default.

# 10. Normative Decision Episode

The primary semantic unit is an episode rather than a person-level score.

## 10.1 Human-readable template

> In context C, toward target T, the person regards action A as required, permitted, optional, discouraged, or forbidden because of reason R, grounded in value V and authority S, given factual premise B, intending outcome O, while accepting or rejecting consequence H, with confidence Q and cost-bearing willingness K.

## 10.3 Semantic safeguards

- `intended_burden` must remain separate from `foreseen_harm`.
- `perceived_outcome` must remain separate from `actual_outcome`.
- `post_event_justification` must not overwrite the prospective intention record.
- `public_response` and `private_response` must remain separately representable.
- inferred motives or intentions must be labeled as inference, not direct report.
- target-group labels should preserve the participant's categorization and a neutral research coding, where appropriate.
- harmful or false beliefs may be represented descriptively without being normalized or endorsed.

# 12. Measurement battery

No single questionnaire should be treated as sufficient.

## 12.1 Instrument 1: Normative orientation interview

Purpose:

- elicit values, rules, loyalties, prohibitions, authorities, and exceptions in the participant's own language;
- preserve emic meaning before standardized coding;
- identify personally salient domains;
- generate candidate scenarios for later testing.

Primary outputs:

- self-defined values;
- principle statements;
- authority sources;
- moralized domains;
- exception rules;
- identity commitments;
- unresolved contradictions.

## 12.2 Instrument 2: Factorial scenario system

Systematically vary:

- target identity;
- relationship;
- in-group versus out-group;
- harm severity;
- intentionality;
- foreseeability;
- legality;
- authority instruction;
- audience;
- scarcity;
- threat;
- personal cost;
- group benefit;
- certainty of evidence;
- power asymmetry;
- role obligation;
- reversibility.

Primary output:

Context coefficients indicating which features change judgment, intention, punishment, tolerance, or willingness to act.

## 12.3 Instrument 3: Trade-off battery

Candidate conflicts include:

- welfare versus loyalty;
- autonomy versus protection;
- equality versus hierarchy;
- truth versus relationship preservation;
- individual rights versus collective security;
- rule consistency versus exceptional need;
- immediate harm versus uncertain future benefit;
- mercy versus deterrence;
- personal integrity versus institutional obedience;
- in-group protection versus universal scope.

Primary output:

Priority thresholds and conflict-resolution rules.

## 12.4 Instrument 4: Public-private divergence protocol

Compare:

- anonymous response;
- identified response;
- in-group audience;
- out-group audience;
- authority-observed response;
- expected behavior;
- intended behavior;
- reported past behavior;
- behavior under opportunity.

Primary output:

A divergence profile that distinguishes strategic presentation, conformity, concealment, uncertainty, and genuine revision.

## 12.5 Instrument 5: Ecological momentary assessment

Brief repeated prompts capture:

- what happened;
- what the person believed was happening;
- what mattered;
- what they felt required or permitted to do;
- what they wanted;
- what they intended;
- what they did;
- what constrained them;
- what followed;
- whether their judgment changed.

Primary output:

Within-person variation and real-world base rates.

## 12.6 Instrument 6: Developmental narrative interview

Trace:

- family rules and discipline;
- early authority relationships;
- religious and cultural learning;
- peer influence;
- experiences of injustice;
- significant transgressions;
- punishment and repair;
- identity transitions;
- ideological conversions;
- disillusionment;
- value conflicts;
- moral injury;
- moments of revision.

Primary output:

A developmental provenance map rather than a deterministic causal claim.

## 12.7 Instrument 7: Epistemic update protocol

Vary:

- source credibility;
- evidence amount;
- consensus;
- ambiguity;
- identity relevance;
- emotional threat;
- group endorsement;
- disconfirming information.

Primary output:

Belief calibration, source trust, identity-protective resistance, and revisability.

# 13. Relationship to personality and other Tessera systems

## 13.1 Personality

Personality is a modulator of expression, not normative content.

Examples:

- conscientiousness may increase consistency with an accepted rule;
- agreeableness may increase relational accommodation;
- threat sensitivity may narrow scope under danger;
- openness may increase exposure to alternative frameworks;
- assertiveness may affect willingness to act publicly;
- emotional volatility may alter behavior under conflict.

None of these traits specifies which ideology, beneficiary, principle, or moral target the person will endorse.

```text
Normative expression = f(
  normative content,
  personality,
  affect,
  cognition,
  identity,
  capability,
  ecology,
  constraint,
  time
)
```

## 13.2 Reconciliation

Reconciliation should examine apparent mismatches such as:

- declared value versus behavior;
- principle versus exception;
- private belief versus public response;
- intention versus capability;
- intended benefit versus actual harm;
- empathy versus restricted scope;
- sophisticated reasoning versus biased premises;
- identity commitment versus new evidence.

Reconciliation does not automatically excuse harm or invalidate the participant's report. It organizes competing explanations for review.

## 13.3 Karma and Ethical Integrity Matrix

The existing 12-dimensional Ethical Integrity and Karma material should be retained as a provisional second-layer overlay.

| Existing dimension | Descriptive redevelopment within TNOA |
|---|---|
| Authentic Presentation | Public-private consistency and strategic presentation |
| Relational Equity | Distributional rule, entitlement, autonomy, and exploitation permission |
| Self-Regard Calibration | Self-priority, status claim, and entitlement structure |
| Material Orientation | Resource and status prioritization |
| Affective Empathy | Detection of and affective response to others' states |
| Accountability Orientation | Responsibility acceptance and attribution pattern |
| Intentional Orientation | Intended beneficiary, intended burden, and accepted harm |
| Moral Reasoning | Justificatory form, scope, and complexity |
| Constraint Internalization | Authority locus and degree of internalization |
| Moral Sensitivity | Consequence detection and concern weighting |
| Moral Courage | Cost-bearing consistency under pressure |
| Development Orientation | Revisability and response to contradiction |

These dimensions should not be automatically summed. A person may display high commitment, courage, empathy, or reasoning complexity in service of harmful aims. The profile and its contradictions are the finding.

## 13.4 Object, cognitive, identity, and behavioral architecture

TNOA fits the Tessera sequence:

```text
Object Architecture
-> Cognitive Architecture
-> Identity Architecture
-> Normative Orientation Architecture
-> Behavioral Architecture
-> Consequence and Developmental Update
```

This ordering is functional rather than permanently fixed. Normative orientation also feeds backward into perception, memory, identity, and future object meaning.

# 14. Scoring and representation policy

## 14.1 No default global score

The initial model should produce profiles, networks, thresholds, and contextual coefficients. A global morality, integrity, or normative-quality score is prohibited unless a later validation program establishes a clear construct and declared benchmark.

## 14.2 Appropriate score types

Depending on the construct, acceptable representations may include:

- categorical status;
- continuous estimate;
- rank order;
- probability distribution;
- threshold;
- network centrality;
- context coefficient;
- within-person variability;
- source-confidence rating;
- contradiction flag;
- qualitative code;
- longitudinal trajectory.

## 14.3 Reflective versus formative constructs

Internal consistency metrics are appropriate only when items are expected to reflect one latent variable. Value profiles, ideology networks, episode configurations, and contextual composites may be formative or network-based and should not be rejected merely because they do not behave like a unidimensional scale.

## 14.4 Inference confidence

Suggested confidence labels:

- Directly reported;
- Behaviorally observed;
- Repeatedly corroborated;
- Context-limited;
- Inferred with moderate support;
- Speculative hypothesis;
- Contradicted;
- Insufficient evidence.

# 17. Development sequence and dependencies

Recommended build order:

1. Values.
2. Principles.
3. Norms.
4. Ideology and factual premises.
5. Morality and moralization.
6. Ethics and justificatory repertoire.
7. Motives.
8. Intentions.
9. Behavior and consequences.
10. Contextual modulation.
11. Developmental and narrative integration.
12. Optional ethical evaluation overlays.

Dependency logic:

- Values precede principles because principles operationalize values.
- Principles and norms must be separated before ideology can be mapped cleanly.
- Factual premises must be separated from values before disagreement is interpreted.
- Motives and intentions must be separated before behavior is used as evidence.
- Context must be measured at every stage rather than appended afterward.
- Ethical evaluation comes last so that descriptive measurement is not bent toward predetermined conclusions.

# 19. Risk register

| Risk | Consequence | Primary safeguard |
|---|---|---|
| Moral evaluation leaks into description | Biased profiles and circular scoring | Keep descriptive, explanatory, and evaluative layers separate. |
| Personality is treated as moral content | Trait-based moral stereotyping | Personality may modulate expression but cannot determine normative content. |
| Behavior is treated as transparent intent | False attribution | Separate motive, intention, control, opportunity, action, and consequence. |
| Context becomes an afterthought | Low ecological validity | Use episode-level and longitudinal person-in-context measurement. |
| Articulate reasoning is mistaken for ethical quality | Rewards verbal skill or rationalization | Code justificatory form separately from scope, premises, beneficiaries, and harm. |
| Internal consistency is overused | Formative or network constructs are rejected | Match psychometrics to construct structure. |
| Automated inference exceeds evidence | Harmful certainty | Preserve provenance, confidence, alternatives, and human review. |
| Cultural variation is mistaken for deficiency | Biased interpretation | Use emic elicitation, invariance testing, and cultural review. |
| Harmful ideology is described without critical boundary | Apparent normalization | Descriptive representation does not equal endorsement; evaluative benchmarks remain explicit. |
| Sensitive identity data is misused | Privacy or discrimination harm | Data minimization, access control, purpose limitation, deletion policy, and use restrictions. |

# Appendix B. Normative Decision Episode example

```text
Context: Workplace team meeting
Role: Project lead
Audience: Direct reports and supervisor
Target: Team member who missed a deadline
Perceived situation: Repeated failure threatens the project
Available actions: Private inquiry, public criticism, reassignment, formal discipline
Normative status: Public criticism judged permissible but not required
Activated values: Efficiency, accountability, fairness
Applied principle: People should answer for preventable failures
Perceived norm: Strong leaders address failure immediately
Ideological frame: Merit and responsibility
Factual premise: The delay was believed to be controllable
Motive: Restore control and signal standards
Intended beneficiary: Project and wider team
Foreseen harm: Embarrassment and reduced trust
Accepted harm: Moderate embarrassment judged necessary
Intention: Confront publicly
Perceived control: High
Behavior: Public criticism
Actual outcome: Compliance increased; trust decreased
Post-event revision: Later evidence showed the delay was caused by an unreported family emergency
Revisability question: Does the accountability principle change, or only the factual premise and chosen means?
```

# Appendix C. Initial research questions

1. Can values, principles, norms, and ideology be measured with adequate discriminant validity?
2. Which contextual manipulations most reliably reveal normative scope and exception rules?
3. How stable are deontic judgments within persons across role, audience, threat, and target changes?
4. Can factual-premise updates change judgment without changing value hierarchy?
5. Which profile features predict intention-behavior divergence?
6. Does constraint load narrow justificatory repertoire or only behavioral options?
7. How do public-private differences vary across culture and power relations?
8. Can longitudinal data distinguish genuine revision from strategic re-description?
9. Which parts of the existing Karma matrix function as descriptive axes, and which require declared ethical benchmarks?
10. What minimum data are required before the system may infer a stable identity-level normative orientation?
